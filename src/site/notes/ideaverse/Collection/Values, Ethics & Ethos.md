## Clarifying the Distinction: “Values” vs. “Ethos”

## Values

- **Values** are fundamental beliefs or principles that guide behavior, choices, and judgment.
- They represent what individuals or groups hold as important, worthwhile, or desirable.
- Values are relatively stable over time and can be personal (e.g., honesty, fairness), organisational (e.g., innovation, accountability), or societal (e.g., justice, freedom).
- They often serve as benchmarks for decision-making and evaluating right from wrong.
- Example: In the context of AI, a value might be *transparency*—the belief that AI systems should be open about their methods and limitations.

## Ethos

- **Ethos** refers to the characteristic spirit, culture, or guiding beliefs of a person, group, organisation, or community.
- It is more than just a collection of values—it is the distinct atmosphere or prevailing philosophy that shapes identity and behavior.
- Ethos is expressed collectively, often through patterns of behavior, shared attitudes, customs, or traditions.
- Ethos provides context—how values are lived out, represented, and fostered within a group.
- Example: An organisation’s ethos might be described as *caring and responsible*—not just valuing transparency, but actively nurturing a culture where openness and accountability are the norm.

---

## In Practice

- **Values are what you believe; ethos is how those beliefs become an embodied, lived reality.**
- For a student exploring AI use:
  - Values might include *integrity* (not cheating) and *curiosity* (desire to learn).
  - Ethos would be the flavour of the academic environment—whether it encourages deep, critical engagement with AI tools or merely compliance with rules.

## Application to Human–AI Collaboration

- In collaborative AI projects (like between Anapoly and Marjon), **values** guide what is important (e.g., ethical use, social benefit), while **ethos** shapes how collaboration feels—whether it is imaginative, respectful, or driven by collective responsibility.
- Imbuing students with “values of benefit to society” means teaching what matters.
- Developing an “ethos of caring” ensures those values shape everyday attitudes and practices, including thoughtful, wise use of AI.

---

**Summary:**

- *Values*: The ideals we prioritise and uphold.
- *Ethos*: The collective character and lived expression of those ideals within a community.

Both are essential for shaping a thoughtful and socially responsible human–AI relationship.

***Perplexity said***

## Ethics vs. Values vs. Ethos

## Ethics

- **Ethics** refers to formal systems or frameworks that define what is right and wrong, usually within a specific context (such as professional, academic, or societal domains).
- Ethics are often codified—set out in rules, guidelines, or codes of conduct. These might be developed by professions, institutions, or societies to guide members’ behavior.
- Ethics involves systematic reflection and reasoning about moral questions—”What *should* we do?” or “What *ought* to be done?”
- Example: In higher education, an ethics code might prohibit plagiarism or mandate the fair use of AI in assessment.

## Values

- **Values** are core beliefs or principles held by individuals, groups, or societies that inform preferences and shape behavior.
- Values are internal and subjective—they are about what people *care about*, such as honesty, creativity, or justice.
- They provide the personal or collective rationale for making choices, but are not necessarily made explicit or written down.
- Example: Valuing *honesty* might guide a student to acknowledge all sources (including AI tools) in their work.

## Ethos

- **Ethos** is the distinctive spirit, culture, or character of a community, organisation, or environment.
- It emerges from how a group enacts its values—through shared attitudes, customs, and habits, often subconsciously.
- Ethos is experienced as the “atmosphere”—is a place supportive, rigorous, open?
- Example: An organisation may have an ethos of *collaborative curiosity*, where asking questions and learning together (including with AI) is encouraged.

---

## In Summary

| Term | What is it? | How is it expressed? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethics | Codified frameworks that define right and wrong in a context | Codes, rules, guidelines, systematic reasoning |
| Values | Core beliefs or principles held by individuals or groups | Choices, priorities, what people care about |
| Ethos | Characteristic spirit or culture of a group or organisation | Customs, atmosphere, “the way things are done” |

- **Ethics**: *The rules or principles defining “what’s right.”*
- **Values**: *What matters most to us.*
- **Ethos**: *The lived spirit, culture, or vibe when we put those values into practice together.*

In the context of the Anapoly–Marjon collaboration, having an explicit ethics framework could guide the responsible use of AI, shared values would clarify what truly matters, and a strong ethos would shape a positive, principled learning environment.

Your statement — *“values are embodied in an ethical framework which is put into practice through an ethos”* — captures the general relationship quite well, but here’s a clearer breakdown:

- **Values** are the underlying beliefs or principles — the “what matters most.”

**Values** → inform → **Ethical Framework** → expressed and reinforced through → **Ethos**.

Your principle — *“Where culture comes before formal ethics, the culture has an implicit, informal ethical framework”* — is reasonable and often true, but it could benefit from some nuance. Here’s a critique:

---

## **Strengths of the Principle**

1. **Recognises ethics beyond codification**
   - It correctly points out that shared values and norms can exist and guide behaviour even when they’re not written down or formally systematised.
   - Many communities rely on “the way things are done” — unwritten rules that still have moral weight.
2. **Acknowledges cultural primacy**
   - In some organisations, the ethos and traditions *precede* any explicit ethics policy, and these traditions act as a de facto framework for behaviour.
3. **Highlights implicit moral guidance**
   - Even without a named “code of ethics,” behaviours are often policed indirectly through social approval, disapproval, and cultural expectations.

---

## **Possible Limitations**

1. **Implied ethical coherence may be overstated**
   - A culture can have traditions and norms without them forming a coherent “framework.”
   - Some cultures are a blend of contradictory influences, where guidance is situational rather than systematic.
2. **Risk of bias and exclusion**
   - An informal framework may simply reflect the dominant group’s values, which may be discriminatory or self-serving rather than universally ethical.
   - Formal ethics often emerge to challenge such biases, not just to document existing norms.
3. **Vulnerability to change or erosion**
   - Without codification, cultural norms can shift quickly, be misinterpreted, or be lost when leadership or membership changes.
   - This fragility means informal ethical systems may lack consistency over time.
4. **Possible absence of moral reflection**
   - A culture might be rich in social customs but poor in explicit moral reasoning.
   - Informal guidance can drift into *habit* rather than being grounded in ethically reasoned principles.

---

## **Refined version of the principle**

You might reframe it as:

> “Where culture precedes formal ethics, it usually operates with implicit moral norms, which may function like an informal ethical framework — though not always coherently, inclusively, or consciously.”

This keeps your core insight but acknowledges that informality can mean inconsistency, unexamined biases, and gaps in moral reasoning.

Alright — let’s weave these strands into a single, coherent explanation that distinguishes **values**, **ethics**, and **ethos**, while recognising the central role of **culture**.

---

## **How Values, Ethics, Ethos, and Culture Connect**

At the foundation are **values** — the beliefs and principles about what is important, desirable, or right. They answer the question: *What matters most to us?*

- Values may be personal (honesty, curiosity), organisational (innovation, accountability), or societal (justice, equality).
- They are the moral “raw material” from which everything else flows.

---

**Culture** is the lived environment shaped by shared history, relationships, language, and traditions. It is *how people actually live and work together over time*.

- Culture naturally expresses shared values but also shapes them — reinforcing some, downplaying others.
- Culture develops whether or not there is any formal ethical guidance.
- When culture comes first, it carries **implicit norms** — unwritten but widely understood expectations of behaviour. These can act *like* an informal ethical framework, but they are not always coherent, inclusive, or consciously examined.

---

**Ethics** is where values become explicit, systematic guidance for behaviour.

- An ethical framework takes values (and sometimes external influences like laws and professional standards) and turns them into principles, rules, or reasoning methods.
- Ethics asks: *Given our values, how should we act, and why?*
- Formal ethics codifies expectations; informal ethics may be embedded in culture without being written down.

---

**Ethos** refers to the characteristic spirit or “feel” of a group — the lived expression of values within a culture.

- While ethics defines the rules and reasoning, ethos captures the *atmosphere* and collective character that emerges when values are embodied in daily life.
- Ethos is tangible in tone, traditions, and shared habits: e.g., a research lab where “integrity and curiosity” aren’t just rules, but felt in how people question, listen, and share.

---

## **Putting it Together**

1. **Values** are the starting point — our moral compass.
2. **Ethics** is a reasoned map that guides how we apply those values in real situations.
3. **Culture** is the social terrain where these values and ethics live, evolve, and sometimes clash.
4. **Ethos** is the *vibe* or spirit of that culture when values are actively lived out.

---

- **Values**: Transparency, social responsibility, curiosity.
- **Ethics**: A joint AI-use policy ensuring clear attribution, fairness, and student agency.
- **Culture**: A collaborative learning environment where open discussion and experimentation are the norm.
- **Ethos**: A spirit of “caring curiosity” about AI — you feel it in workshops, discussions, and documentation.

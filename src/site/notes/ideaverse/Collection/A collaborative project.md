# A collaborative project?

Transcript of a chat between Alec Fearon and ChatGPT 5 on 9 August 2025

---

***Alec said***

`<reference>`Marjon University is interested in a collaboration with Anapoly. The collaboration promises to extend the capability of both organisations for the social good. `<reference>`Students are among those most at risk from the misuse of AI. `<reference>`There is a risk that students might outsource their thinking to AI and lose or not acquire the ability for critical thinking. `<principle>`There is a need to imbue students with values of benefit to society. `<principle>`Students need to have an ethos of caring about how the human-AI relationship evolves. `<process>`An LLM can be configured to support students to become wiser. `<concept>`Anapoly Notebook will have an area in which philosophical ideas can be explored. `<task>`Discuss

***ChatGPT said***

Here’s how the elements you’ve listed fit together, and where the “LLM to support wisdom” question becomes central.

---

## 1. Collaboration premise

Marjon University and Anapoly are both interested in AI for social good.

- Marjon brings access to students, an at-risk group in terms of AI misuse.
- Anapoly brings structured experimentation methods and public transparency.
- The shared value space is about *enhancing capability without eroding human judgement*.

---

## 2. Risk frame

- Students may “outsource” their thinking to AI, which risks weakening critical faculties.
- The misuse risk is not just about plagiarism or misinformation — it’s about *habits of mind*.
- In your own frameworks, this parallels the **acclimatisation → experimentation → proof-of-concept → iteration** lab stages, but with a moral and developmental overlay.

---

## 3. Guiding principles

From your notes:

- **Value formation**: embed values that benefit society, not just skills for employability.
- **Ethos of stewardship**: care about the human–AI relationship as something to be shaped, not passively accepted.  
  These align with NIST’s idea that human-AI interaction should be *goal- and outcome-oriented* and that context of use (technical, social, cultural) is part of the design space.

---

## 4. Process implication — “LLM to support wisdom”

If the LLM is to support students in *becoming wise* rather than just *becoming efficient*, configuration needs to be deliberate. That could mean:

- **Prompt scaffolds** that force reflection before answer consumption (e.g., “What reasoning steps did you take before consulting me?”).
- **Bias and value checks** embedded in interactions.
- **Structured content** that guides the AI to emphasise principles, trade-offs, and ethical consequences, not just factual answers
- **Review stages** (your Lab Framework’s “Review” and “Wrap-up”) where human participants articulate learning and value implications, not just outcomes.

---

## 5. Conceptual integration — Anapoly Notebook’s philosophical area

- The philosophical space could act as a *sandbox for wisdom-support configurations*.
- It would host experiments in *value-aware prompting*, *critical-thinking patterns*, and *ethics-in-context scenarios*.
- Publishing these experiments — with your transparency labelling and structured lab notes — could both demystify the work and invite peer contribution.

---

## 6. Discussion points for you and Marjon

1. **Scope of “wisdom”** — Is it moral reasoning, long-term perspective, integration of knowledge, or something else?
2. **Measurement** — How will we know if an LLM interaction genuinely promotes wiser thinking?
3. **Configuration approach** — Do we embed guardrails and prompts in the LLM, or do we teach students to use general-purpose models in wise-making ways?
4. **Risk mitigation** — How do we ensure “wisdom-support” does not become disguised persuasion or value-imposition?
5. **Sustainability** — How will the philosophical area in Anapoly Notebook be maintained, curated, and used in teaching and research?

---

If you want, I can map these into a **pilot lab design** showing how to test “LLM-for-wisdom” with students under your existing Lab Framework, including setup, roles, and asset flow. That would make it operational rather than aspirational.

***Alec said***

To begin with at least, I think I’d like the philosophical space to focus on the guiding principles of value formation and ethos. Indeed, we might perhaps call that space values and ethos. Please comment on that.

***ChatGPT said***

Calling the space **Values and Ethos** is sharper and easier to grasp than “philosophical space.”

**Advantages:**

- **Plain English** — avoids sounding like an abstract academic corner.
- **Signals purpose** — makes it clear this is about *normative grounding*, not idle speculation.
- **Invites participation** — “values” and “ethos” are terms students and practitioners can relate to, even if they wouldn’t enter a “philosophy” section.
- **Maps to your stated principles** — it directly reflects the two you’ve prioritised: *values of benefit to society* and *caring about the human–AI relationship*.

**Potential refinements:**

- Decide whether this space is **exploratory** (asking “what values should guide us?”) or **declarative** (asserting “these are our values”). The tone and format will differ.
- Consider whether to frame it as **Values & Ethos in Human–AI Interaction** — that ties it directly to your mission and helps prevent it from drifting into generic moral commentary.
- If it becomes a standing area in Anapoly Notebook, align it with your **lab note structure** and **transparency framework** so that each entry is both open-ended and methodically documented.

If you want, I can outline a **content template** for Values & Ethos posts that keeps them grounded, repeatable, and linked back to your lab process. That would stop it from becoming a loose collection of opinion pieces.

***Alec said***

Yes, it will be the values and ethos in human-AI interaction. The end product will be a long-form essay, and the philosophical space, the values and ethos space, will be the home for a project to develop that long-form essay. My feeling is that this might fit the academic approach that the university might like. It could be something that students would benefit from participating in. What are your thoughts about all this?

***ChatGPT said***

I think you’re right — framing it as a **project in the Values & Ethos space** with the goal of producing a long-form essay does several useful things.

**Why it works for Marjon and students:**

1. **Academic compatibility** – A defined research-to-publication pathway will feel familiar in a university setting. It echoes dissertation or capstone work, but in a collaborative and experimental format.
2. **Student engagement** – Gives them a stake in shaping the final product, rather than just reading about it. They’d contribute insights, literature reviews, use-case critiques, and possibly their own “values tests” of LLM interactions.
3. **Structured rigour** – If you wrap it in your Lab Framework, you get planning, operation, review, and wrap-up phases. That produces an audit trail and deliverables Marjon can reference.
4. **Ethical literacy** – Students don’t just hear “AI has risks”; they practise articulating *what values matter* and *why*, and apply them to concrete human–AI interaction scenarios.

**How I’d suggest shaping it:**

- **Working Title** – *Values & Ethos in Human–AI Interaction: A Collaborative Inquiry*.
- **Method** – Treat it as a series of mini-labs inside the project, each exploring one principle (e.g. transparency, reciprocity, autonomy).
- **Roles** – Facilitator (Anapoly), Explorers (students), Quality & Trust Steward (faculty or Anapoly lead to ensure coherence and academic integrity).
- **Assets** – Each mini-lab produces a lab note, feeding into sections of the final essay.
- **Transparency** – Apply your existing classification labels so readers see where AI contributed to the thinking or drafting.

**Benefits:**

- For Marjon: a tangible, citable output demonstrating responsible AI pedagogy.
- For students: co-authorship or acknowledgment in a public, high-quality piece of work.
- For Anapoly: a tested format for running “wisdom-support” labs in other contexts.

If you like, I can map this into a **Values & Ethos project plan** using your Lab Framework so you can present it to Marjon as both academically respectable and operationally concrete.

***Alec said***

We must remember that the long-form essay, the end result, is important in its own right and is a key purpose for the work. Equally important is the fact that we’re using that end product as a vehicle for learning how to make use of AI in a real-life setting, which was, of course, the original purpose of Annapolis AI Labs.

***ChatGPT said***

Yes — that dual purpose is the strength of it, and it’s worth making explicit from the outset:

1. **The essay as a scholarly contribution**
   - Stands alone as a thoughtful, well-researched piece on *Values & Ethos in Human–AI Interaction*.
   - Has lasting value for Marjon, Anapoly, and a wider audience.
2. **The essay as a vehicle for applied AI learning**
   - Every step — research, drafting, review — becomes a live case study in how to work productively and critically with AI.
   - Students and faculty experience the Lab Framework in action, not just as a concept.
   - Transparency labels and lab notes document *how* AI contributed, creating a meta-layer of insight.

If you frame it this way when pitching to Marjon, you avoid the trap of the essay being seen as just an “output” or the process being seen as just “teaching AI skills.” You’re showing how a real intellectual task can be both:

- **a product worth publishing**, and
- **a process worth replicating** in other academic or professional contexts.

I’d suggest we write the project brief with *two explicit objectives*:

1. Produce a long-form essay that is a credible academic/public contribution.
2. Demonstrate and document the effective, ethical use of AI in a collaborative research and writing process.

If you want, I can draft that **two-objective project brief** in your Lab Framework style so it’s ready to drop into a Marjon proposal. That way, they see the scholarly goal and the applied-learning goal as equal, deliberate parts of the design.

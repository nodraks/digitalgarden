# The power of the counter-question

*Transparency label: AI-only*

Written by Gemini Notebook in the Safety Senior notebook, from Frank Delaney's videos, and copied here on 5 September 2026 with its source citations removed. Delaney, a former fraud investigator, runs the YouTube channel Safety Senior. The five rules from the same channel are in [[ideaverse/Collection/Five rules for answering an unknown call\|Five rules for answering an unknown call]], and the case against the word "yes" is in [[ideaverse/Collection/The dangers of the yes trap phone scam\|The dangers of the yes trap phone scam]].

Answering **"Who is calling, please?"** (or simply **"Who's calling?"**) is infinitely safer and more effective than saying **"yes"** because it completely flips the power dynamic of the call.

This small shift in your vocabulary makes you nearly impossible to scam for several key reasons:

### 1. It Lowers Your "Responsiveness Score"

Scammers operate like organized businesses, and they evaluate your behavior in the first 30 seconds of a call to determine if you are a profitable target. They track a metric that fraud investigators call a **"responsiveness score"**.

* When you answer reflexively with **"yes"**, your score shoots up because you have demonstrated that you are polite, cooperative, and willing to engage. This marks your number as a high-value target, and your number will likely be bundled and sold to other scam operations on the dark web.
* When you answer calmly and ask **"Who is calling?"**, you receive a **low responsiveness score**. Scammers hate wasting time on uncooperative targets and will quickly move on to the next number.

### 2. It Disrupts Their Script

Phone scammers are workers reading from highly rehearsed scripts designed to keep you in a passive, reactive state. The entire scam relies on them leading the conversation while you provide the answers.

Asking **"Who is calling, please?"** triggers what psychologists call **script disruption**. It forces the caller to step completely outside their playbook. A scammer is not prepared to explain themselves or face an investigation, and reversing the questions often causes them to stutter, get defensive, or simply hang up.

### 3. It Instantly Filters Out the Criminals

Legitimate callers, like your doctor's office, pharmacy, or bank branch, will immediately and transparently identify themselves, their organization, and their reason for calling in their very first breath. When you ask **"Who's calling?"**, a real caller has your file in front of them and will answer immediately.

A scammer, however, is often auto-dialing thousands of numbers at once and usually doesn't even know who they have reached. When pressed with a direct question, they will hesitate, give vague answers, or try to redirect control back to their script by asking you questions first.

### 4. It Denies Them Vocal Weapons

Saying "yes" is a massive liability. It gives scammers recorded verbal consent that can be spliced into automated systems to authorize fraudulent charges, or clean audio material to feed into AI voice-cloning software.

Answering **"Who is calling, please?"** gives them **zero confirmation**. It provides no affirmative "yes" recording, confirms nothing about your identity, and denies them the high-quality, predictable audio clips they need to target you or your family.

### The Line in the Sand

Your rule of thumb on any incoming call should be: **they verify to you, never you to them**. Since they placed the call, the burden of proof is entirely on them. If a caller refuses to let you verify their identity, or tries to pressure you to stay on the line, that resistance is their confession: hang up immediately.
